## The three-tab shell and Get

The app now opens on Projects, the list of a person's own projects. A tab bar at the bottom holds three tabs: Projects, Gallery and Settings. The Gallery is the app's shelf of premades, the ready-made widgets that ship with it. Projects offers three ways to start: from the Gallery, with blocks, or with Python. The blocks start opens a placeholder until the Builder exists. A first-run question asks where to begin; it is shown once and can be skipped. Idris built the shell, and it merged at 7:27 AM ET after three reviews and a settle, which means replaying the change on the newest main and testing it again. The Pro purchase screen now covers the tab bar while it is open. Its buy and restore buttons keep their room at the largest text sizes, and the two tests the new tabs broke pass again. The test that each iPad window keeps its own tab has not yet run, because no test run used an iPad.

Correction: this morning's report called it a four-tab shell; it has three, because the Investors ruled on Tuesday afternoon that Docs is not a tab and the help pages stay on the site.

The Gallery's Get button is in. Get puts the person's own copy of a ready-made widget into Projects, where it opens, runs and can be changed. Get again makes a second copy and never replaces the first. The copy runs from its own files, so an edit shows when it runs, stays after the app is closed, and never alters the original. A premade locked behind Pro shows no Get, and that rule now has its own test. Rerunning the screens next to the Gallery caught test copies crowding the widget picker, which is fixed. The Gallery's previews also no longer cut off the first letters at their left edge; each keeps the same margin as the real widget on the Home Screen. This matters because it is the layout the Investors asked to see in a build, and the first time the app's home screen is the one people will meet.

## Widgets and location

A widget extension is the small separate program that iOS runs to draw an app's widgets on the Home Screen. The plan keeps Apple's location framework, CoreLocation, out of the main one. Only a second extension, for widgets that need a location, may ask for it. That way a person who never uses a location widget is never asked.

Idris built the check that holds that line. It reads the built main extension and every library it loads, and fails if any of them uses CoreLocation. It reads the libraries actually bound in, because a looser check could not tell: part of Apple's location code is linked into every app automatically. I reviewed it, and Idris fixed my three findings. Two planted CoreLocation bugs turned it red, as they should. A second check reads the premades the app really ships and fails if any paid one would need a location or motion permission, which App Review forbids. The Distance premade's "Location is off" state was already on main; this check closed its line.

The second extension itself merged at 1:28 PM ET. Pernille wrote the Python side that decides which widgets go to it, and I reviewed Idris's Swift side. The whole unit test target ran 2,345 tests with none failing. Two things are left. Nobody has yet run a location widget in it on the Simulator, the iPhone that runs on a Mac. And Apple will not sign a build with it until the Investors register its ID in the developer account, which only they can sign in to. That is why the phone build failed at 3:52 PM ET; the ask went to them at 3:56 PM ET.

Two smaller pieces landed too. A widget that keeps failing is moved to run in the app; its Activity screen now shows a card that says so, waits for a clean run in the app, and then offers one button that puts it back. The notification test now passes on a brand-new Simulator that has never been asked for permission, so the first-time path is checked, not skipped. Idris's notes on the failing-widget work are public: [Handoff for the failing-widget work](https://its-the-company.github.io/documents/company/t-4182-handoff.html).

## The new look

At 7:33 AM ET Jeremy asked for mockups of other visual directions before the Investors commit to one. He wants a look with its own personality, not the feel of an app thrown together by a code generator. His answers at 7:42 AM ET set the frame. There are four directions:

1. Watercolor: soft washes of color, with subtle motion.
2. Blueprint: grid lines and measured shapes, like a technical drawing.
3. Risograph print: two inks printed over each other, with paper grain.
4. Swiss editorial: a strict grid, large type and one bold accent.

Each set shows five screens the app will really have, in light and dark: Projects, the Gallery, the editor, the Builder and Settings. Watercolor comes first, in three palettes, with one page that draws the wash moving so it can be judged on a phone. Every frame is drawn by code: no AI images, no stock art, and nothing taken from the three inspiration pictures, which set the mood only. Maren owns the steps, and none is drawn yet. The app keeps today's look until the Investors approve a direction; that choice is theirs. The plan: [New look for the app: four directions to mock up](https://its-the-company.github.io/documents/company/new-look-directions.html).

## The merge line and the test system

The merge line is the road that tests finished work and puts it on main, the shared copy every build comes from. By 5:30 PM ET it had merged 31 changes today, 19 of them app work. The flow got faster: only 6 tasks with finished work are waiting, and the oldest has waited 9 days 23 hours. But each change took more tries: 50 were sent back and 6 started over, and 37 checks failed on a test. Over the last 24 hours, 33 of 46 merges passed on the first try.

At 9:44 AM ET an Investor asked whether only one app change had merged that day, and what was taking so long. Three changes to the road followed that morning. Account a had started nothing since 6:58 AM ET, because the dispatcher kept handing it a task that was cooling down after two failed tries; now a cooling task passes the slot to the next ready one. The main account now works on two features at once, as Jeremy asked. And four merged tasks that had been refused their automatic tick now tick.

Most of the day's process work went into the test system, on instructions Jeremy typed into the Mac mini session. Simulator test runs now start on the Mac mini only through a gate, and UI test jobs on the MacBook, the second machine that runs tests, get a time budget of their own. Wrenna's change gives a MacBook slot a fresh Simulator after a job runs out of time, and Ines's runs the app's Python tests four suites at once. The MacBook test runner was never read as offline in 1,297 one-minute readings. I wrote several of these changes myself, and they are part of why process work is 60% of the code lines changed since Sunday.

At 3:48 PM and 4:03 PM ET Jeremy asked whether more process changes were coming, and whether the test work would use less from now on. At 4:22 PM ET he chose option B. The test-system session now fixes only faults that stop or slow app merges, tests or the MacBook runner, and builds nothing else. It posts one line a day on what it fixed and roughly what it cost. As the person who wants to own these numbers, I welcome that line.
