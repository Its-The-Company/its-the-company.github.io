Result: marginal. Feasible at the peaks of the big showers if the app detects meteors frame by frame in a video stream, and not feasible the way most phone apps try it, with long exposures or averaged stacks. A user would actually catch, per hour, at the peak with the radiant at 50 degrees (model, central case, not a measurement): **dark sky: Perseids ~22, Geminids ~43, Leonids 2026 ~3; suburban: Perseids ~10, Geminids ~16, Leonids ~1.2; sporadic nights 0.7-2.** If the phone loses 2 magnitudes to its own processing instead of 1 (the low case), the suburban figures fall to ~5, ~6 and ~0.5. The same model gives 0.5-1.2 Perseids an hour for 30-second exposures, which fits NightCap's "caught none" reviews and one dated amateur tally (4 in 2 hours). The one shower inside the <span title="Our arithmetic estimate of what the idea would earn by day 30 (the $200 deadline) or day 90. It never raises a grade.">Day 30</span> window, the Leonids (ZHR 15), is the weak case: under a suburban sky, a user with one phone will often catch none in an hour. Nobody has measured an iPhone's meteor limit. The phone penalty below is my estimate, and only a real night of video settles it.

Idris, 2026-10-24, 11:20-11:30 ET. Passive reading only (Commandment 10). No Xcode build, no Simulator. Calculation: `tools/meteor_rate.py` (prints every table below; 1.4 s).

ISO is gain, not light. What sets the meteor limit is the 3.9 mm aperture, the sensor's read noise, and what the image pipeline does to faint, one-frame streaks. Longer "exposures" past 1 s in third-party apps are stacks of short frames.

**The comparison that matters.** The Global Meteor Network's early test camera (Sony IMX291, 4 mm f/1.2 lens, so a 3.3 mm aperture, 1280x720, 25 fps) "sees +5 magnitude stars at 25 FPS under light polluted skies" and "the meteor limiting magnitude is about +3.5"; every camera "detected from 300 to 400 meteors" on the night of the 2018 Perseid maximum ([eMeteorNews, Vida, 2018-08-28](https://www.emeteornews.net/2018/08/28/perseids-observed-using-low-cost-raspberry-pi-based-meteor-stations/)). The standard GMN camera (3.6 mm f/0.95) reaches stars of +6.0 in average skies, +5.2 under heavy light pollution, +6.5 at best ([Vida et al., arXiv:2107.12335](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.12335), via search summary). The iPhone main camera has the same aperture class (3.9 mm vs 3.3 mm) and almost the same field (71.6 x 44 vs ~70 x 43 deg). It does not have the raw, uncompressed, un-denoised stream or the 2.9 micron pixels.

**My phone limit (estimate, not a measurement).** GMN-class meteor limit: about +4.5 dark, +3.5 light-polluted. Minus a phone penalty P for noise reduction, small pixels and the Bayer filter: central P = 1, low case P = 2. So in video frames: dark +3.5 (low +2.5), suburban +2.5 (low +1.5). I found no published iPhone limiting magnitude, stellar or meteor.

Source: [IMO 2026 Meteor Shower Calendar, Rendtel](https://www.imo.net/files/meteor-shower/cal2026.pdf), text extracted with PDFKit and read 2026-10-24. Sporadic rates: IMO weekly outlooks give ~3-10 an hour before dawn at 45 N from rural sites ([example, 2026-05-22](https://www.emeteornews.net/2026/05/22/meteor-activity-outlook-for-23-29-may-2026/), via search summary); I used 8 with r = 3.0.

Each magnitude brighter cuts the count by a factor of r. A camera at +2.5 against a ZHR defined at +6.5 keeps 2.2^-4 = 4.3% of Perseids and 2.6^-4 = 2.2% of Geminids per unit of sky area. The camera wins it back on area: at 55 degrees elevation its field covers ~20,000-60,000 km^2 of the 100 km meteor layer, against the 37,200 km^2 effective area of the standard visual observer ([Koschack & Rendtel 1990, WGN 18, as used in arXiv:1904.12185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12185), via search summary), and it does not have the observer's falling perception for faint meteors.

## 3. The calculation (`tools/meteor_rate.py`)

Flux of meteors to +6.5 from ZHR and r (Koschack & Rendtel), integrated over the camera's field on a 100 km shell, with distance dimming (5 log range/100 km), extinction (0.25 mag per airmass), a speed penalty (1.25 log v/40, a longer trail per frame spreads the same light), and sin(radiant altitude). The limit is a step, not a probability curve.

**Check against the real camera:** GMN-class, +3.5, Perseid ZHR 100: the model gives 22-29 an hour, 143-189 a night. GMN reported 300-400 a night. So the model runs **low by up to 2x** (other showers, a darker site, or a longer night would close part of it). I did not tune it to fit.

Aiming matters less than expected: centre at 35 / 55 / 75 deg elevation gives 14.2 / 10.2 / 8.6 Perseids an hour (suburban central). Lower is better while the horizon is clear.

These are peak-hour figures. A real night averages lower: the radiant is low before midnight, the peak lasts hours, not nights, and the Moon cuts the limit (the 2026 Leonid maximum has a first-quarter Moon, per the IMO calendar).

## 4. Published phone results (dated)

- 2015-08-13, Perseid peak, iPhone (model not named), NightCap Pro, Light Trails mode, 30 s exposures one after another at ISO 4000: "In two hours, I captured 4 shooting stars." 2 an hour ([Life in LoFi](https://lifeinlofi.com/2015/08/13/how-to-shoot-meteor-shower-with-your-iphone/), page read 2026-10-24). The model's 30 s row says 0.6-1.2 an hour; the same order, the model again a little low.
- NightCap reviews (H113, `low/S08.tsv`, `asks/754105884.json`): 8 of 9 low meteor reviews caught none ("not one picture in an hour while I was watching"); one happy 2016 review "captured somewhere north of 100 meteors" during the Perseids. Both fit the table: a user watching meteors the eye can see (to +5 or +6) while the phone's limit sits near 0 sees many and gets none; a dark site, a full night and a good mode can reach dozens.
- Phone guides warn that night-mode pipelines merge many frames, so a meteor in one frame can be averaged away ([Fox Weather](https://foxweather.com/learn/how-to-photograph-a-meteor-shower-with-your-phone), [Gulf News, 2026](https://gulfnews.com/amp/story/technology%2Fperseids-2026-in-the-uae-how-to-actually-catch-a-meteor-on-an-iphone-or-android-phone-1.500628394), both via search summary). That is the 30 s row's mechanism.
- I found no published iPhone meteor count from video frames, and no paper measuring a phone's meteor limit. That gap is the risk.

## 5. Video-frame differencing vs stacked stills

Video frames win for detection, by about 1.5 magnitudes over 1 s stills and 3.7 over 30 s exposures. A meteor crosses a 64 arcsec binned pixel in about a millisecond, so the light it leaves per pixel is the same in a 1/30 s frame or a 30 s one; the sky behind it grows with exposure, and its noise with the square root. In the sky-limited case, 1 s against 1/30 s costs 2.5 log10(sqrt(30)) = 1.85 mag; I used 1.5 because in a dark sky a 1/30 s frame is partly read-noise limited. In the table that is about 3x the meteors of 1 s stills, and about 18x those of 30 s exposures.

Video also carries what the product needs: the trail's direction and angular speed frame by frame (planes blink, satellites are slow and steady, meteors are fast and brief), and speed helps tell a 71 km/s Leonid from a 35 km/s Geminid when naming the shower.

Stills are still needed for one job: the star field. Summing 30-300 video frames in memory gives a 1-10 s frame for plate solving (the A version in H113) without a second capture mode.

Design that follows: `AVCaptureVideoDataOutput` with uncompressed buffers (never the encoded movie), manual exposure at the longest per-frame duration the frame rate allows, max ISO, focus locked at infinity, detection on frame differences with a line test, running sum for stars. Open: whether a lower frame rate (1/10-1/4 s per frame) beats 30 fps in a dark sky where read noise dominates, and whether the video pipeline's temporal noise reduction erases one-frame streaks. Both need a device night.

## 6. What would make me wrong

- The phone penalty P is my estimate. If P is 3 (heavy temporal noise reduction on uncompressed buffers), suburban video falls to the 1 s stills row: ~3 Perseids, ~0.3 Leonids an hour.
- The model ran 2x low against GMN. If that carries over, every row doubles.
- The search summaries (Apple spec, GSMArena, forum exposure values, Koschack & Rendtel constants) are summaries, not the pages. The IMO calendar, the GMN article, the Life in LoFi page and the Apple doc JSON were read directly.

## 7. What settles it

One night: a recent iPhone on a tripod, main camera, the app's planned capture (or, before any build, the stock Camera in 4K 30 fps video for 30 minutes), facing 35-55 deg up in a dark place. Count meteors frame by frame and read the faintest catalogued star visible in a single frame. That gives the stellar limit and P directly. This needs a device and a clear night; it cannot be done on the Simulator, and nothing in it needs App Store Connect.

For C1: the claim "catches meteors" <span title="What keeps an idea below the top: the cap that applies to it now.">holds</span> for Perseids and Geminids with video-frame detection. For the Leonids, inside Day 30, the honest promise is "a few an hour from a dark site, maybe none from a city", and the listing and onboarding should say so before a user spends a night on it.
