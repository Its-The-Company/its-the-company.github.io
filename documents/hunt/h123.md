Result: yes at night, yes in the mountains by day, and the compass alone is not good enough for either. **Plate solving a single iPhone frame should reach about 0.05-0.1 deg (my scaling of a published 10 arcsec figure; nobody has published a phone number), with 36-46 catalogued stars in the main camera's field at the stellar limit H122 implies, a 15-50 MB Apache-2.0 database, about 3-4 weeks. Skyline-to-DEM matching reaches 5 deg on 80% of mountain photos in one published test and about 0.2 deg in another (my conversion of a 3-pixel figure), on ~40 MB tiles under a free Copernicus licence, about 6-8 weeks, and only where the horizon has relief. The compass and CoreMotion reach 1-3 deg just after calibration and 2-3 times worse after that, with single readings 13-25 deg off; it costs nothing and is the starting guess for both other paths.** astrometry.net, the best-known solver, is GPL-3 as a whole and cannot go into a closed App Store app; tetra3 and cedar-solve (Apache-2.0) can. ## (a) Plate solving an iPhone night frame

**Field of view.** Apple's iPhone 17 Pro spec page lists "48MP Fusion Main: 24 mm, ƒ/1.78" and "48MP Fusion Ultra Wide: 13 mm, ƒ/2.2 aperture and 120° field of view" ([Apple Support 125090, iPhone 17 Pro Tech Specs, read 2026-10-24](https://support.apple.com/en-us/125090)). Apple states no angle for the main camera. My arithmetic from the 24 mm equivalent (43.27 mm diagonal, 4:3 frame): 71.6 x 56.8 deg (84 deg diagonal) in photos, 71.6 x 44 deg in 16:9 video (the figure H122 used). The ultra-wide's 120 deg is diagonal; at that width distortion correction becomes most of the solver's problem, so the main camera is the solving camera.

**Stars to H122's limit.** H122 gives a meteor limit in video frames of about +2.5 suburban and +3.5 dark (central case), and GMN's cameras see stars about 1.5 mag deeper than their meteor limit (+5 stars, +3.5 meteors). So, as an estimate: stars to about +4 suburban and +5 dark in a single video frame, one magnitude less in H122's low case; a 1 s still goes about 1.5 mag deeper (H122's frame arithmetic). Counted from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue ([VizieR V/50, catalog.gz, downloaded 2026-10-24](https://cdsarc.cds.unistra.fr/ftp/V/50/)), averaged over the sky (my arithmetic; the field covers 8.97% of the sphere in 4:3, 7.03% in 16:9):

A lost-in-space solver needs a handful of stars to form a pattern and a few more to confirm it. At +3 (H122's low suburban case) there are 12-16; at +4, 36-46. Star count is not the risk; haze, clouds, a lit foreground and lens distortion are.

**Solvers and licences.**
- astrometry.net: the LICENSE file says the team's own code is 3-clause BSD, "HOWEVER, since this code uses libraries licensed under the GNU GPL (including a vendored GSL), the whole work must be distributed under the GPL version 3 or later" ([LICENSE, main branch, read 2026-10-24](https://github.com/dstndstn/astrometry.net/blob/main/LICENSE)). A closed App Store binary cannot meet GPL-3 (full corresponding source, no added restrictions); under Commandment 6 we do not ship it. Its index files are data, but using them means reimplementing the solver from scratch.
- Index sizes (astrometry.net 4100 series, Tycho-2): quads 1400-2000 arcmin (4119) down to 340-480 arcmin (4115) ([doc/readme.rst](https://github.com/dstndstn/astrometry.net/blob/main/doc/readme.rst)); the files 4115-4119 are 740,160 + 408,960 + 247,680 + 187,200 + 144,000 bytes = 1.73 MB ([data.astrometry.net/4100/, listing read 2026-10-24, files dated 2013-03-05](https://data.astrometry.net/4100/)). The whole series down to 4107 is ~350 MB; a 70 deg field needs only the widest few.
- tetra3 (ESA): Apache-2.0; "in general solutions will take 10 milliseconds (excluding time to extract star positions from images) with 10 arcsecond (50 microradian) accuracy"; default database "built for 10 to 30 degrees with stars down to magnitude 7" ([esa/tetra3 README, last push 2024-08-14](https://github.com/esa/tetra3)). The default database is 49,414,085 bytes (GitHub tree API, read 2026-10-24). No hardware is named for the 10 ms.
- cedar-solve (fork of tetra3, used in PiFinder-class devices): Apache-2.0, last push 2026-08-06; default database 14,655,563 bytes ([smroid/cedar-solve](https://github.com/smroid/cedar-solve), tree API read 2026-10-24). I found no README speed figure.
- tetra3rs, a Rust port: MIT ([docs.rs/tetra3](https://docs.rs/tetra3), via search summary).

**Phone solve times.** None published. AstroHelm (launched May 2026, iOS and Android) says it plate-solves on the device offline and claims "up to 10 arcseconds" through a telescope ([ScopeTrader](https://scopetrader.com/iPhone-plate-solving-app-AstroHelm-launches/), via search summary); no timing. FinderCam with Plate Solving is on the App Store ([id1541018483](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1541018483)); no timing. Gap: solve time and accuracy on an iPhone frame. My expectation, not a measurement: star extraction on a 12 MP frame dominates, well under a second on a current iPhone; the pattern match is milliseconds.

**Attitude error (estimate).** tetra3's 10 arcsec is for ~10 deg fields at ~512 px. The iPhone main camera spreads 71.6 deg over 4032 px, 64 arcsec a pixel, and its lens is not calibrated by us. Centroids to a few tenths of a pixel give a few tens of arcsec; uncorrected distortion over a 70 deg field adds more. My estimate is 0.05-0.1 deg once each camera model is calibrated, and worse without. That is 20-60 times better than the compass. It needs a clear sky and a frame of the sky; it says nothing indoors or by day.

## (b) Compass and CoreMotion as published

- Apple, `CLHeading.headingAccuracy`: "The maximum deviation (measured in degrees) between the reported heading and the true geomagnetic heading... A negative value means that the reported heading is invalid, which can occur when the device is uncalibrated or there is strong interference from local magnetic fields" ([Apple docs, read as JSON 2026-10-24](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/clheading/headingaccuracy)). It is a run-time bound; Apple publishes no typical value. `headingFilter` defaults to 1 degree ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/cllocationmanager/headingfilter)).
- Apple, `CMAttitudeReferenceFrame.xTrueNorthZVertical`: needs a magnetometer and Location Services (for declination); "If the magnetometer isn't currently calibrated, Core Motion prompts the person to move the device to calibrate it" ([docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmattitudereferenceframe/xtruenorthzvertical)). CoreMotion's heading therefore carries the same magnetometer error.
- Measured: Tartachynska & Pylypyak, "Use of smartphones for determining orientation angles in the field", Geodesy, Cartography and Aerial Photography, posted 2026-05-28, theodolite reference: "the first magnetic-azimuth measurement after compass calibration has the smallest error, typically 1–3°. In subsequent measurements without repeated calibration, the error increased on average by a factor of 2–3... the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of azimuth determination in most cases did not exceed 2°... in isolated measurements, significant deviations of up to 13–25° were recorded" ([science.lpnu.ua/node/42954](https://science.lpnu.ua/node/42954), abstract read 2026-10-24). The abstract does not say which phones.
- Older: six iPhones against a Suunto compass, some up to 20 deg off, worst on iPhone 5 ([GeoGarage blog, 2013-10](https://blog.geogarage.com/2013/10/the-iphones-positioning-sensors-were.html), via search summary; not peer-reviewed). Allmendinger, Siron & Scott 2017 (J. Struct. Geol., 3,700 iPhone measurements) call iOS devices fit "for all but the most demanding applications" with care near metal ([ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/structural-data-collection-with-mobile-devices-accuracy-redundanc/), via search summary); I did not get their degree figures.
- Pitch and roll: a 2013 smartphone compass-clinometer comparison found 1.7 deg mean dip difference outdoors ([AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65dfd6cd0f3e94b9e5dcab44), via search summary; phone not stated). Gap: no published iPhone CoreMotion pitch/roll error.

For a sky or peak overlay, 2 deg is about four full Moons. Good enough to start the search, not to put a label on a star or a summit.

## (c) Skyline-to-DEM matching

**Data and licences.**
- Copernicus DEM GLO-30. Licence (COP-DEM-GLO-30-F): "The rights granted under this Licence are worldwide and without limitation in time"; rights of "(a) reproduction; (b) distribution; (c) communication to the General Public; (d) adaptation, modification and combination with other data"; "free of charge"; the user shall show "© DLR e.V. 2010-2014 and © Airbus Defence and Space GmbH 2014-2018 provided under COPERNICUS..." ([licence PDF, Articles 3-6 read 2026-10-24](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/APIs/SentinelHub/Data/DEM/resources/license/License-COPDEM-30.pdf)). Articles 3-6 contain no commercial exclusion; I did not read the rest of the PDF, so the attribution notice and disclaimer go to a human check before any ship. Accuracy: absolute vertical < 4 m LE90, relative < 2 m on slopes ≤20% within a 1x1 deg area ([EMD wiki, Airbus figures](https://help.emd.dk/mediawiki/index.php/Copernicus_DEM), via search summary). Tiles: 1x1 deg Cloud Optimized GeoTIFFs; three on the public AWS bucket measured 41.8 MB (N46 E007, Alps), 38.9 MB (N40 W106, Colorado), 48.2 MB (N40 W075) by HTTP HEAD, files dated 2022-05-09. Uncompressed 3600x3600 float32 is 51.8 MB (arithmetic).
- SRTM GL1: 3601x3601 16-bit cells per 1x1 deg tile, ~25.9 MB uncompressed; free worldwide since November 2014; 16 m absolute LE90 spec, RMSE 9.73 m in a v3 summary ([NASA SRTM v3 guide via OpenTopography](https://www.opentopodata.org/assets/NASA_SRTM_V3.pdf), [OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Srtm), via search summaries). Licence: the OSM wiki calls it public domain; the [data.gov record](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/shuttle-radar-topography-mission-1-arc-second-global) says no licence information was provided. Gap: a first-party licence line. GLO-30 is the cleaner choice anyway: a written grant and better accuracy.

**Published accuracy.**
- Lefèvre, Tuia, Wegner, Produit, Nassar, "Towards seamless multi-view scene analysis from satellite to street-level" (arXiv:1705.08101, 2017-05-23), Swiss photos, 25 m DEM: "on a set of 100 images, both the horizon matching and the textured model matching were able to recover 80% of the azimuth within 5°"; failures from weather and hidden peaks; needs a starting position within 1 km (PDF read 2026-10-24).
- Nicolle, Bonneton, Konik, Muselet, Tougne, "Towards an Electronic Orientation Table", VISAPP 2017, pp. 28-38, smartphone photos on SRTM 90 m: mean skyline distance 3 px against ~18 px for the baseline, at 1024x768, ~20 images, 0.5 s per image on a 1.3 GHz Core i5; they widen the search because of "the lack of precision of the compass" ([SciTePress](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2017/61157/pdf/index.html), via page summary). No degrees and no field of view given; 3 px is ≈0.2 deg if the field was ~65 deg wide (my conversion).
- Products: PeakLens (Politecnico di Milano) says matching the camera to an SRTM panorama corrects "most errors" of compass and GPS (store listing, via search summary); PeakVisor does similar. No published error figure from either.
- Brejcha & Čadík, 3DV 2018 (semantic cues, GeoPose3K) and Baboud et al., CVPR 2011 (photo-to-terrain): found, numbers not read (the MPI PDF returned 403). Gap: a published heading error in degrees for skyline matching on an iPhone.

**Where it works.** Mountain and hill country, clear day, horizon in frame. In flat land, in a city, at night, or in fog there is nothing to match, and the app must say so instead of guessing.

## What this means for S1 and the sky family

- Night: plate solving is the strong path and the data is small. The compass gives the starting guess and the camera corrects it to a tenth of a degree. This is also the engine the meteor catcher (H122) and a sky identifier would share.
- Day: skyline matching is the only path past the compass, and only with relief. Expect partial success (80% within 5 deg in the one published test) unless our matcher is better than 2017's.
- The compass alone (2 deg typical, 13-25 deg outliers) cannot carry a "true north" claim. Any feature that shows a direction must show its error bar, and refuse when headingAccuracy is negative.

## What would make me wrong

- A phone night frame may not reach +4: H122's phone penalty is an estimate. At +2 there are only 4 stars in the field and the solver fails. One clear night with a recent iPhone and the stock Camera settles it (same night as H122's test).
- Lens distortion on the main camera could hold the solve to ~0.2-0.5 deg until we calibrate each model; AVCameraCalibrationData exists but I have not checked which formats deliver it.
- The 0.2 deg skyline figure is my conversion of a pixel number with an unstated field of view. The 5 deg / 80% figure is the only one in degrees.

## Sources not used

The 2024 Journal of Emerging Investigators calibration paper (1.37 deg heading after a custom calibration) is a student paper on one phone; noted, not used in the table.
