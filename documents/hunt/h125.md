Result: on the Leonid night of 2026-11-17 there is no such sigma in the 0.5-10 degree range. "Likely Leonid" is right 96.8% of the time at sigma 10 degrees (isotropic error, IMO naming rule, speed check on; 3,413 Leonids and 1,632 sporadics among 8,000 simulated detections) and stays at or above 80% out to 30 degrees. Attitude error costs missed Leonids, not wrong names: with the IMO rule, 80% of Leonids are still named at sigma 6.2 degrees (87.6% at 5, 75.0% at 7). The 80%-right line only bites in a weak case (Leonid ZHR cut to a third, no speed check): sigma 8.4 degrees (tight rule) to 10.9 (IMO rule) for an isotropic error, 13-25 for a compass-heading error. Model, not a measurement.

Tool: `tools/radiant_sim.py` (Monte Carlo, pure math, no network). Tests: `tests/radiant_sim_test.py`, 33 checks, one seeded fixture (`tests/radiant_sim_fixtures/leonids_n300_seed7.json`), wired into `tests/run.sh`. Author: Odalys, 2026-10-24.

## Findings

1. Peak Leonid night, speed check on (n = 8,000; 3,413 LEO, 1,632 sporadic): "likely Leonid" right 98.6% at sigma 0.5, 96.8% at 10, 87.9% at 30 (iso error, IMO rule). Under the sigma-aware tight rule with a compass-heading error, 94.5% of Leonids are named at sigma 10 and the label is right 97.1% of the time; the cost is sporadics wrongly named, 9.7% at 0.5 rising to 37.2% at 10.
2. Why precision holds: Leonids are 43% of what the camera catches that night in this model (H122 rates: ZHR 15, r 2.5 against a sporadic rate of 8, r 3.0, camera limit +2.5), and their radiant is one small target.
3. The speed check matters less than expected at the peak: with it off, the 80% line still holds out to 30 degrees for all four mode/rule pairs (n = 8,000). It matters in the weak case: at a third of the Leonid ZHR, turning it on moves the isotropic crossing from 8.4-10.9 degrees to 20-21.
4. Geminids 2026-12-14 (n = 8,000; 7,524 GEM, 279 sporadic): "likely Geminid" right at least 97.8% at every sigma to 30. With the IMO rule, 80% of Geminids are named at sigma 5.6 degrees (84.4% at 5, 70.2% at 7); the tight rule under a heading error keeps 93.3% at 10.
5. Error type matters more than error size. A compass-heading error (tilt held to 0.5 degrees by gravity) costs far less than the same sigma about every axis: with the IMO rule at sigma 10, 87.8% of Leonids are named under a heading error against 55.9% under an isotropic one (same 3,413 Leonids). A phone's tilt is good; its heading is the weak part. What the app should report and test is the heading error.
6. Design consequence: an app that knows its own attitude error should widen its association window with it (the tight rule). With a fixed IMO window, recall falls fast past sigma 5; with the widened window it holds above 90% to sigma 10 for a heading error, at the price of more sporadics given a shower name.

## Weaknesses (read before using the numbers)

- The speed check assumes the angular speed is measured to 5% and the meteor height is known to about 10%. A 30 fps phone frame sees a Leonid move ~1 degree per frame; that is plausible but not measured.
- Sporadic radiants: 30% apex (in mid-November ~10 degrees from the Leonid radiant, fast), 25% antihelion, 45% spread. That mix and scatter are my assumption, not an IMO table.
- One attitude error per meteor; a real session has one bias for the whole night, so a single user's night can be much worse or better than the average shown.
- ZHR tapers linearly from the Table 5 maximum; no Moon (the 2026 Leonid maximum has a first-quarter Moon per the IMO calendar), no cloud, no detection losses beyond a 1.5 degree minimum track.
- Radiant drifts are typical values, not read from IMO Table 6 (its text did not survive extraction); the shift is under 1 degree for the two target showers on these nights.
- Precision depends on the base rate. If the phone's meteor limit is worse than +2.5, Leonids fall faster than sporadics only if r differs (2.5 vs 3.0 favours the shower); the --zhr-scale 0.33 run is the stress case.
