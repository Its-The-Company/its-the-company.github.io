Result: **marginal.** Detection works in the published literature, but the only published precision of 80% or more comes after a CNN trained on a network's own labelled archive: the Spanish Meteor Network reports **98% precision** (Peña-Asensio et al. 2023). The classic detector that RMS grew from, on a clear moonless night, produced **53 false detections for 14 meteors in about 9.1 h: 5.8 false an hour, 21% precision**. The CAMS detector on the same night produced 18 false for 11 meteors: 2.0 an hour, 38% (Vida et al. 2016, Table 1). Neither classic figure meets the H124 bar (1 false an hour or fewer, or 80% precision). The CNN figure meets it on paper, but only for a fixed allsky camera with a trained classifier. A phone on a tripod adds insects, tree motion, plane and Starlink exposure and phone-pipeline noise, and no source gives rates for these. My script on the one public clip pair I may use (NASA allsky, one confirmed fireball seen by two cameras) caught **0 of 2** with fixed defaults and raised **1 false detection**, on NASA's own overlay: **precision 0 of 1.** Two 2-second clips cannot give a per-hour rate.

Idris, 2026-10-24, 11:35-11:43 ET (stamps from `date`). No Xcode, no Simulator. Clips downloaded to ~/data/H128/ (a new, empty directory outside the repo), treated as untrusted, read by a script kept in tools/. Script: `tools/meteor_detect.py` (about 5 s per clip).

## 1. Published methods and licences

**GPL and the App Store (Commandment 6).** RMS is GPL-3.0. Copying or translating its code into a closed app sold on the App Store would oblige us to release our source under GPL-3.0. Apple's usage rules also add restrictions GPL forbids, which is why GPL apps have been pulled from the store before (VLC, 2011, from general knowledge; not re-read today). So we use no RMS code. `tools/meteor_detect.py` follows the method as the two papers describe it: thresholds, morphology, Hough, temporal line, speed range. Methods are not covered by copyright. I wrote it without opening RMS source. Any production port must be written the same way and checked by a human before it ships. CAMS is proprietary, so it is out.

## 2. False-positive sources a phone on a tripod meets


<div class="tbl" role="region" tabindex="0" aria-label="Table 2: 2. False-positive sources a phone on a tripod meets, scrolls sideways" style="--tw:960px" markdown="1">

| Source | Published rate | Note |
|---|---|---|
| All non-meteor triggers, classic detector, clear moonless night | **53 per ~9.1 h = 5.8/h** (new RMS-precursor), **18 = 2.0/h** (CAMS) | Vida 2016 Table 1, night 1: 3,211 files of 256 frames at 25 fps |
| Moonlit fast clouds, partly clear | **1,679 in one night** (new), 304 (CAMS) | Vida 2016 Table 1, night 3 |
| Rain in light pollution / storm with lightning | 36 / 0 (new), 2,941 / 318 (CAMS) | Vida 2016 Table 1, nights 4 and 5 |
| Starlink trains | no rate; "a significant uptick in the number of false meteor detections on satellites"; around dawn "about half of all 150+ GMN meteor cameras observe a train" | GMN blog, 2019-11-25 (via search summary) |
| Planes, birds, bats | no rate; named as CAMS false triggers | Silađi et al. 2015 (via search summary) |
| Cosmic-ray hits, hot pixels | no published phone rate found | single-frame, single-blob: the 4-frame rule removes them; my script also drops blobs under 3 px |
| Insects near the lens | no published rate found | blurred and fast; they pass the speed gate. A phone at ground level meets them more often than a roof camera |

</div>


Planes, satellites and Starlink fail the 2-51 deg/s gate or the duration rule only if they are seen for long enough. A plane crossing a 72-degree phone field takes tens of seconds, so it is rejected. A plane strobe shorter than 4 frames is dropped. A satellite flare of 0.5-1 s is the hard case. Satellite rejection by orbit (CelesTrak) is described only by hobbyists (BAA forum, via search summary). Its licence is H129's.

What the NASA clips are: event clips only, each about 2 s (64-67 frames at 29.97 fps, 640x480), cut around a detection the network has already confirmed. They test recall on bright fireballs. They cannot measure a false rate per hour, because the hours between events are not published. I found no public full-night allsky video with labels.

## 4. The run (`tools/meteor_detect.py`)

Clips: `ev_20261020_052251A_17A.mp4` (Hiram) and `ev_20261020_052251A_16A.avi` (Oberlin), from fireballs.ndc.nasa.gov/20261021.html. One event, 2026-10-20 05:22:51 UTC, seen by two stations and given an orbit (evcorr 20261020_052251C). Ground truth: 1 meteor per clip, at the spot NASA's own brackets mark on the max-pixel image (17A near x 461, y 55, about 12 px long; 16A near x 301, y 23, a few px). Defaults fixed before the run: K = 4, J = 8, blob ≥ 3 px, Hough threshold 15, min line 10 px, 0.4 deg/px.

Why it missed: in a fisheye allsky frame this fireball is a 12 px streak, shorter than the Hough vote of 15. I did not retune on these two clips, because that would only fit the answer. A phone's 72-degree field <span title="What keeps an idea below the top: the cap that applies to it now.">holds</span> the same meteor at roughly 3-4 times the pixel length (about 0.11 deg/px against about 0.4), so this miss does not carry over directly. Neither does the 0-of-1 precision. What the run does show: overlays and timestamps are the first false source, and a phone app has its own (the preview UI is not in the frame, but HDR and noise-reduction steps are).

## 5. What this means for Slice A (H124 rule 1, K-A1)

- Not "not": the method is published, runs in seconds on a short clip, and needs no licensed code. K-A1 does not fire.
- Not "feasible" on the classic detector: 2.0-5.8 false an hour on the best night, and 21-38% precision.
- "Feasible" only if Ines counts the SPMN CNN's 98% (an allsky network with a trained classifier) under rule 1. A phone version needs its own labelled training set, and we have none: no public negative footage, and GMN raw clips are not released.
- Open: one device night, recorded raw, hand-labelled (needs a device, after Day 7); a recall test on more NASA event clips with parameters fixed in advance (the archive has clips most nights); the SPMN paper's full text for recall and per-hour figures (only the abstract and a search summary were read).
