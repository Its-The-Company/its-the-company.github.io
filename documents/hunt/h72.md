## Feasibility: where subtitle text can come from (docs and terms only)

- **OpenSubtitles API: no (not established).** www.opensubtitles.com/en/tos and www.opensubtitles.org/en/disclaimer answered with a Cloudflare "Just a moment..." check (HTTP 403) and /en/consumers 403'd to the fetch; that is a human check, so I stopped (C6). The API docs at opensubtitles.stoplight.io load by JavaScript and served no text. Third-party pages disagree on the free quota (10, 5, 100 or 200 downloads a day) and show a consumer form with a "Personal use" type; none of that is the owner's text, so I quote none of it as terms. Apple's side is written: guideline 5.2.2, "If your app uses, accesses, monetizes access to, or displays content from a third-party service, ensure that you are specifically permitted to do so under the service's terms of use. Authorization must be provided upon request." Without readable terms there is no permission to show. An Investor reading the terms in a browser could turn this into a yes or a firm no.
- **Platform subtitles: no.** Netflix Terms of Use 1.4: "for your personal, non-commercial use only"; 1.8: "Unless otherwise explicitly authorized, you agree not to: ... archive, reproduce, distribute, modify, display, perform, publish, license" and "use any robot, spider, scraper or other automated means to access the Netflix service". YouTube Data API captions.download: "This method requires the user to have permission to edit the video." Apple 5.2.3: no ability "to save, convert, or download media from third-party sources ... without explicit authorization from those sources".
- **User-supplied .srt: yes, for one file at a time.** The app reads a file the user picks and computes coverage on the device; it fetches nothing from a third party, so 5.2.2 and 5.2.3 do not read on it as written, provided the app does not point users to download sites (5.2.3: "Apps should not facilitate illegal file sharing"). It answers "is this file at my level", not "which show should I watch": the user must already hold the subtitles of every show to be compared.
- **Learner side: Anki yes, test open.** Anki exports "Notes in Plain Text" (fields split by tabs) and deck packages where "Include Scheduling Information" keeps or drops scheduling (Anki manual, Exporting). Known versus still-learning needs the scheduling, so it means reading an .apkg, not the plain text. A short frequency-band test: I read no validated test for Spanish, French, German or Japanese in this session; open. The sample says it must be measured: 0 of 30 askers know their word count.

## Coverage studies (only what I read)

- Ha, H. T. (2022). "Vocabulary Demands of Informal Spoken English Revisited: What Does It Take to Understand Movies, TV Programs, and Soap Operas?" Frontiers in Psychology 13. Corpus of 626 million words (75,000 TV episodes, 25,000 movie scripts, 22,000 soap episodes): 3,000 BNC/COCA word families for 95% of scripted dialogue and 5,000 for 98%, counting proper nouns, marginal words, transparent compounds and acronyms as known; movies and TV differ by "approximately 0.2%". On per-title spread: it "was not possible for the researcher to go deeper and explore the variation in lexical coverage among texts".
- Webb and Rodgers (2009), TV and movies: **read only as Ha's Table 1 reports them**, 3,000 word families for 95% and 7,000 (TV) or 6,000 (movies) for 98%. I did not read the original papers; sources disagree on which is 2009a and which 2009b.
- Webb, S. (2011). "Selecting Television Programs for Language Learning: Investigating Television Programs from the Same Genre." International Journal of English Studies 11(1), 117-135. 288 episodes; same-subgenre episodes held fewer word families and recycled 4,000-14,000-level words more; "watching programs within the same subgenre may be an effective approach to language learning with television."
- All three are English. I read no coverage study for Spanish, French, German or Japanese; Migaku's blog figure for Japanese Netflix (1,442 words for 80%) reached me through a search summary; the post was not opened and the number is the vendor's.

What this means for the product: the averages say most shows need about the same vocabulary, and the one study that could show per-title spread says it did not measure it. The ranker's premise (titles differ enough at the learner's level to be worth ranking) is plausible from Webb 2011 but unmeasured in anything I read.

## What would make me wrong

- A subtitle licence I did not find (an OpenSubtitles commercial tier whose terms permit analysis in a paid app) would <span title="A new, permitted reading that removes a cap. A lift rule is that reading, written down before we look.">lift</span> concept 1's Day 90, not its novelty: Migaku is already there.

Rows 86, 144 and 156 of its pull are continuation lines of titles holding a newline, so tally.py skips them and misses the B code of rows 85, 143, 155: 33 builder posts, tally prints 30.
