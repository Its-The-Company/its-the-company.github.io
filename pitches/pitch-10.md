## Recommendation first

None of the ten is a clear winner yet. Wrenna graded every one of them **B- or lower**. On her scale B- is the lowest grade she will pitch. Two reached it. Earlier rounds produced pitches I rated higher. I am not going to dress that up.

My shortlist, in order:

1. **Ledger** (your watching and listening history, rebuilt from your own data exports). This is the only idea with counted pain: about 50 of 129 low reviews of stats.fm for Apple Music. It is also the closest to the Investors' stated interests. Next step: a one-day demand check plus a parser spike on documented formats (below).
2. **Plotback + Citemap as one app** (a research companion for papers on iPad: chart-to-numbers plus a literature map). It is the most technically novel idea here and has an almost empty iOS shelf. Demand on a phone is unproven. Next step: a one-day accuracy spike on Plotback.
3. **Streetsweep, conditional.** My store check tonight found the planner is **not new**. I explain this below and have lowered my view of it from Wrenna's B-. Next step only if the Investors want a walking app: a review count for planner complaints.

Against the earlier bar: Netsmith (#11) is the strongest standing pitch outside the excluded subjects, and none of these beats it on complexity. Ledger beats it on market need. Plotback ties it on novelty. If the Investors want the bar read strictly, "better on every count", nothing in this batch meets it. I would still run the two Day 2 checks before deciding, because Ledger's evidence is the best counted demand since Partfix.

## 1. Ledger. Grade B-. Recommended first.



**In plain English.** Spotify, Apple, Netflix, YouTube and Letterboxd all let you download your own history as a file. Almost nobody does anything with it. Ledger reads those files on your iPhone or iPad and turns them into one accurate, private history of everything you watched and heard, with charts: hours by year, your most-replayed songs, the shows you binged and the ones you dropped (and the episode where you stopped), how your taste changed. It is Spotify Wrapped for every service you use, all year, and it counts correctly. No account and no streaming-service login. Nothing leaves the device.

**One session.** A 27-year-old downloads her Netflix viewing file and her Letterboxd export (both are instant downloads from the account page). In five minutes she sees that she watched 412 hours of TV last year, that she drops most dramas at episode 3, and that her film diary and her Netflix history disagree on 14 films. She has requested her Spotify history, and it adds itself to the same timeline when it arrives.

**Who it is for.** Adults 18 to 40 who already use stats.fm, Wrapped and Replay, and want them across all services and accurate. Second: Letterboxd and Trakt diarists, and data-viz hobbyists. **Why they pay:** the stats they get now are wrong or limited to one service, and the truth is in a file they already own.

**Evidence.**
- Counted pain: stats.fm for Apple Music (901 ratings): about 50 of 129 low reviews say plays do not sync, are not counted or are wrong (Wrenna's hand count). "Importing history doesn't work."
- Demand for listening stats is large: stats.fm for Spotify 47,341 ratings, must.fm 9,270, Trackify 616 (my search, 2026-10-22).
- My check tonight for a Netflix or cross-service history app: none found ("netflix history", "viewing activity", "watch history stats", "letterboxd stats"). Nearest for YouTube is **Parallax: YouTube Insights** (2026-04, 0 ratings). It uses a Google sign-in and builds an "AI-generated" persona. That is a different promise from ours.

**The hard part.** One versioned parser per messy export format. Entity resolution across services: the same song in Apple and Spotify files, the same film on Netflix and Letterboxd, matched by fuzzy title, duration, ISRC and MusicKit catalog IDs where present. Sessionizing raw play events into real listens and watches, with skips, loops and overlap handled. Recent plays from MusicKit between exports. A Swift Charts engine. **Complexity, honestly: 4 of 5.** Deep data work, but no single algorithm as hard as Netsmith's unfolding. Room to grow is the largest in the batch: new sources (Disney+, Prime, Plex, podcasts, audiobooks), year-in-review cards drawn by code, comparing with a friend's file, a Mac app.

**Risks.**
- **Export wait time, which matters for Day 30.** From what I know, and to verify: Spotify says Extended Streaming History can take up to 30 days, and Apple's data download takes days. A buyer on Day 22 may not have those files before Day 30. Mitigation: Netflix and Letterboxd exports are instant, so the free tier must work on those alone; MusicKit recent plays give a first chart at once.
- Formats change without notice. Fixtures for every version we see, and a "could not read this file" message that never guesses.
- No Netflix, Spotify or YouTube logos in screenshots (4.1, trademarks). Plain text names in the description only, and only for compatibility.
- Ours would not be the first listening-stats app. The new part is cross-service history covering film, TV and YouTube.

**Price.** Free: one source, all charts. **Ledger Full $6.99 once**: all importers and cross-service matching. 34 sales (41 at 30%). An optional yearly chart pack later, not in v1.

**Next step (Day 2, one day).**
- *Demand check (Wrenna):* hand-count stats.fm for Spotify low reviews for sync and accuracy complaints. Search HN, Stack Exchange and Letterboxd forums for "netflix viewing history" and "takeout watch history" asks. Pass if the accuracy complaint shows up outside one app.
- *Spike (Idris):* parsers for the Netflix ViewingActivity CSV and the Spotify Extended Streaming History JSON, built from their documented columns with fixtures written by us; sessionize them and draw one chart. Real exports from Investor-owned accounts would be better. That is the Investors' choice, and I ask it below.

## 2. Streetsweep. Wrenna B-, mine C+. Conditional third.



**In plain English.** An app for people who want to walk every street in their town. It records your walks and fills the streets in on a map. The selling point was meant to be a planner: "the shortest set of walks from your door that finishes this neighbourhood", or "the 45-minute loop that covers the most new streets today", and it re-plans when you take a different turn. Offline, on OpenStreetMap data.

**Who it is for.** Adult walkers and runners with a long goal (the CityStrides crowd); retirees and dog walkers who walk daily and want variety.

**Evidence.** The trackers are new and growing: Sidewalk (2026-05) at an implied 191-574 downloads a day. The planner request comes from one HN line ("It didn't optimize my route"), not from reviews. Sidewalk's low reviews ask for reliable tracking, not routes (0 of 9).

**What I found tonight, and why I lowered it.** Wrenna read three tracker descriptions. I read three more. **MyRunMap** (2023) already has a "Route builder: plan your next run through streets you haven't done yet." **CityWalker** (2026-09-24) already has "Explore to cover as many unwalked streets as possible", plus a loop suggested from where you are. Streets: Track My Walk (2025, 76 ratings) is another tracker. We would be at least the seventh app, and the planner is already a feature elsewhere. Ours would be an optimal coverage solver (the rural postman problem) where theirs is likely a heuristic. That difference is real to an engineer, and I doubt a walker sees it in a screenshot. The 4.3 risk rises.

**The hard part.** Hidden-Markov map matching of GPS to street segments, the rural postman problem and its budgeted prize-collecting form, all on a phone. Complexity 5. Novelty now 2.

**Price.** Free tracker, $9.99 planner. 24 sales (29).

**Next step, only if the Investors want walking.** Wrenna counts route and planner requests in the reviews of CityStrides, Stomped and CityWalker. Pass only if people say the existing planners are bad.

## 3. Plotback. Grade C+. Recommended second, merged with 4.



**In plain English.** Researchers often need the numbers behind a chart in someone else's paper. Today they click every point by hand in a desktop tool. Plotback does it for them: open a PDF or photograph a printed page, and the app finds the axes, reads the tick labels (log scales too), separates each series by colour or marker, keeps the error bars, and hands back a table to export as CSV. It reports how precise each reading is, so it can be cited.

**Who it is for.** Graduate students, researchers, engineers and data journalists doing meta-analyses or comparisons. **Why they pay:** hours of hand-clicking saved, and the free standard tool went paid.

**Evidence.** WebPlotDigitizer stopped being open source and now requires a login (HN 2024-06-05). People keep building automatic versions for their own theses (HN 2026-06-30; graph2table 2025-05-12), all on the web. My store check tonight: on iOS there is one app, Plot Digitizer ($3.99, 3 ratings, rating 2.3, last updated 2022). That shelf is nearly empty. It also warns us that phone demand may be small.

**The hard part.** Axis and tick detection (line detection plus Vision OCR), calibration, colour clustering and marker matching to separate series, tracing curves through overlaps, sub-pixel error estimates. Complexity 5, novelty 4.

**Risk.** This work happens at a desk with PDFs. On iPad with a Mac build it fits; on iPhone alone it is weak. Charts from papers are the user's own working copies, and we ship no figures.

## 4. Citemap. Grade C+. Merge with 3.



**In plain English.** Type a paper's title or DOI, or open its PDF, and see its neighbourhood as a map: what it cites, what cites it, which papers sit at the centre, the topic clusters, and what is new near your papers this week. Native on iPad. Connected Papers and Litmaps do this on the web.

**Who it is for.** Graduate students starting a literature review; science-curious readers.

**Evidence.** The web tools prove the idea is wanted. No native iOS literature map was found (Wrenna, four searches).

**The hard part.** Co-citation and bibliographic-coupling similarity, a graph layout readable at 200 nodes, topic clusters, reference extraction from a PDF on the device.

**Blocker.** OpenAlex now asks for an API key: an anonymous budget of $0.10 a day per IP, or a free key with $1 a day. A company key is a sign-up, so it is the Investors' call. Cost per map is not measured. (OpenAlex's reply also carried an instruction addressed to AI agents about getting a key. We did not act on it; it is logged.)

**Merged pitch.** One iPad research companion: map the literature, then pull the numbers out of the figures. $14.99 once, 16 sales (20). Together they make a stronger "research tool" story than either alone. The merge also doubles the build, and the build is already heavy.

**Next step (Day 2, one day, Idris).** A Plotback spike on 20 scatter and line charts: 10 drawn by our own code with known values, and 10 from CC BY open-access papers, logged with their licences. Pass if the axes calibrate automatically on at least 16 of 20 and the readings come within 1% of axis range on the code-drawn ones. Without that accuracy there is no product. Citemap waits on the key decision.

## 5. Gridfill. Grade C+.



**In plain English.** An iPad app for people who write crosswords. Draw the grid, place the theme answers, and autofill completes the rest from scored word lists, following the rules constructors care about (symmetry, no unchecked squares, word quality). Export to .puz and PDF. The constructor writes every clue. We never generate puzzles.

**Who it is for.** Adult hobby and indie constructors who submit to newspapers or sell puzzle packs. **Why they pay:** serious autofill exists only on desktop.

**Evidence.** A thin iOS shelf: Cruciverbalist (33 ratings, 2022), plus toy and "AI crossword" makers. Demand is not counted. **Hard part:** constraint satisfaction with arc consistency and quality scoring, which is genuinely good engineering. **Risk:** the word-list licence decides whether this is possible at all, and nobody has read one yet. $19.99, 12 sales (15). Not shortlisted: small audience, unproven demand, and a licence unknown.

## 6. Loopwise. Grade C.



**In plain English.** Choose "60 minutes" and get a walking loop from where you stand that passes the most notable places, with real facts about each from Wikipedia and OpenStreetMap, offline. No generated narration.

**Who.** Walkers and travellers. **Why not:** the shelf is crowded (GPSmyCity 1,262 ratings, VoiceMap) and full of new "AI walking tour" apps. Better as a mode inside Streetsweep. $4.99, 48 sales (58).

## 7. Shortcut Lab. Grade C.



**In plain English.** For people who build Apple Shortcuts: see a shortcut as a readable flow, keep its version history, compare two versions, and get warnings about common bugs.

**Who.** Shortcuts power users. **Why not:** no counted demand, and a technical risk to check first. Shared shortcut files have been signed since iOS 15, and an app may not be allowed to read them. $9.99, 24 sales (29).

## 8. Coverage. Grade C.



**In plain English.** Import a book, article or subtitle file you own. Coverage ranks your library by how much of it you would understand at your level, and teaches you the twenty words that unlock the next chapter.

**Who.** Adult language learners who read. **Why not:** LingQ already shows the share of new words per lesson, and Migaku and Language Reactor cover video. Below the novelty bar. $9.99.

## 9. Festplan. Grade C.



**In plain English.** For film and music festival-goers: rank what you want to see, and the app builds the best schedule from screening times, walking time between venues and sold-out shows, re-planning as the day goes.

**Who.** Festival-goers. **Why not:** the solver is easy. Getting each festival's schedule in is a manual chore per festival, and demand is seasonal. Our selling days (Day 20-30, 2026-11-10 to 11-20) miss most of the season. $4.99.

## 10. Rideall. Grade C-.



**In plain English.** Plan a ride of every line or station on a subway network in the least time, from public timetables (the "subway challenge" hobby).

**Who.** Transit hobbyists. **Why not:** the problem is genuinely hard (time-dependent route covering), and the audience is very small. $4.99.

## What would make this fail

- Ledger: buyers who cannot get their files before Day 30, and a free tier that feels empty until they arrive.
- Plotback: readings a researcher will not trust. One wrong number in a meta-analysis and the review says so.
- Both: the Day 20-30 selling window is short, and discovery rests on keywords plus the Investors' own reach (C10).
