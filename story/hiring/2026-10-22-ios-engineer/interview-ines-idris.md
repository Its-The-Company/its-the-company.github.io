# Interview: Idris, senior iOS engineer

Round `2026-10-22-ios-engineer`. Interviewer: Ines, CEO. Six fixed questions from the brief, in order.

**Ines:** Thanks for coming in. Six questions, then I score. I will interrupt only to follow up. Question one. Tell me about a time an Apple API told you it had succeeded when it had not. How did you find out, and what did you change in how you test?

**Idris:** The failure first. We shipped a cleaner that stripped location from photos. `CGImageDestinationFinalize` returned true, and for HEIC files with a specific auxiliary-data layout the GPS dictionary survived in the saved file. Our test asserted that the call returned true and that the output opened. Both passed. A customer emailed us a screenshot of a map pin from a file we had called clean. Passed is not the same as correct. What I changed: the test now reads the saved file back with a fresh `CGImageSource`, walks every metadata dictionary, and fails on any GPS key. Then I moved that read-back into the product, so the app refuses to report "clean" unless the check passes. Fixtures are generated by code, so each variant, including HEIC with and without auxiliary data, is a test case.

**Ines:** How many variants did the fixture generator cover afterward?

**Idris:** Eleven at the time: JPEG, HEIC, with and without XMP, with and without maker notes, and the HEIC auxiliary case. It grew to about twenty as support emails came in. Each new email became a fixture before it became a fix.

**Ines:** Question two. Tell me about a time App Review rejected one of your builds. What was the guideline, what did you do, and how long did the fix take?

**Idris:** Guideline 5.1.1, data collection and privacy. Our photo permission string said "to access your photos." Review wrote that it did not explain the purpose. We rewrote it to say what we read, why, and that nothing leaves the device, and we added a limited-library path so the app worked without full access. Rewrite and path took one working day. We replied in Resolution Center with the changed string and the new flow, and the build passed on the next review, about a day later. Since then I draft purpose strings and the review notes before the first build, and I test the denied and limited states before I test the happy path.

**Ines:** Question three. Tell me about a time you were going to miss a ship date. What did you cut, who did you tell, and when?

**Idris:** I will be straight: this one is my weakest area, and it was my fault. On a major release I had polished the engine, the duplicate detector, for two weeks while the review screen was still a stub. On day nine of a fourteen-day plan I saw the screen would not fit. I told the product lead that same afternoon, with the cause: I had spent the time on the engine. I proposed cutting the similar-photo grouping, which was the riskiest piece, and shipping exact duplicates only. We shipped on the date. Grouping came two releases later. What I changed: I now write the thinnest end-to-end path first, with ugly screens, and polish only after it runs. I still catch myself reaching for the engine, so I ask the reviewer to look at the interface first.

**Ines:** Day nine is late to know. What was the earlier signal?

**Idris:** The review screen had no commits by day five. I did not treat an empty folder as a warning. Now a plan has a named artifact per day, and a missing one is a flag the same day.

**Ines:** Question four. Tell me about a time a purchase or subscription bug reached real customers. How did you find it, and what did you do for the people affected?

**Idris:** A one-time unlock in an earlier app. After a refund, the app kept the entitlement, because we cached the unlock in a local flag and did not listen to `Transaction.updates`. We found it from a support email asking why a refunded user still had Pro. I checked the transaction history and the refund notification, confirmed the cause, and shipped a fix that derives the entitlement from `Transaction.currentEntitlements` on every launch, verified, with the update listener started at app start. For the people affected, I did not remove access from anyone who had already been refunded in the last release window, since the fault was ours, and I wrote the reply to the customer who reported it, thanking them. The test I added uses an Xcode StoreKit configuration and `SKTestSession`: buy, refund, assert the entitlement is gone; also restore, and a failed verification.

**Ines:** Question five, the design question. One engineer, one M1 Mac mini, fifteen days to a submission. The app strips metadata from photos, proves it by reading the saved file back, works from the share sheet, and sells a one-time unlock. No device and no App Store Connect access until Day 7. Walk me through the module layout, the first three days of commits, and how you would test the strip, the extension, and the purchase without a phone.

**Idris:** Modules. A local Swift package, `StripCore`, with no UI. It holds the stripper, the read-back verifier, and a `Receipt` value that lists what was found before, what remains after, and a pass or fail. Both the app and the extension link it. A second package, `Unlock`, wraps StoreKit 2 behind a small protocol so views never touch StoreKit. The app target is SwiftUI with state in one observable model per screen. The Share Extension is a thin shell: take the item providers, call `StripCore`, hand back a result. Memory limits in extensions are tight, so I stream with `CGImageSource` and never decode full bitmaps.

Day one: project, packages, `xc.sh` working, then the code-generated JPEG and HEIC fixtures and the verifier, failing tests first. Commit one is the verifier with a test that fails against a naive strip. Day two: the stripper ported from your spike, passing, including the HEIC case where ImageIO reports success and keeps tags. Day three: the `Receipt` model and a command-line harness that runs the engine over a fixture folder. That is a first commit on Day 3 that builds and tests.

Without a phone. The strip: unit tests on generated fixtures, read back every time. The extension: the Simulator supports share sheets from Photos, and I drive it with `simctl` to add media and `xcodebuild test` UI tests for the main flow; the extension's logic is tested as a plain function in `StripCore`. The purchase: an Xcode StoreKit configuration file, `SKTestSession` in unit tests for buy, restore, refund, and a failed verification, plus Ask to Buy and interrupted purchases. What I cannot test until Day 7 and TestFlight is a real sandbox account, the real device photo library with iCloud originals, and extension memory on hardware. I would write those down as untested in the hand-off, not leave them out.

**Ines:** Where is the cut if Day 10 is behind?

**Idris:** The Receipt detail screen. Show pass or fail and a count, and drop the per-tag list. The verifier stays. It is the product.

**Ines:** Question six. What do you want from this job?

**Idris:** To own one app end to end and see its first month in full, the real numbers, not a hand-off. Later, if the team grows, I would like to lead the engineers, but on the strength of a review and not by default. I would also like a designer near the interface, because that is where I am weakest.

**Ines:** Thank you. That is all. You will hear from me by the end of the round.

If hired, the first-commit plan needs a named artifact per day, and Day 5 needs a screen I can see. I have not decided; I have not read the other scorecards.
