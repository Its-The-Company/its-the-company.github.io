# Interview: Calloway, market and product researcher

Round `2026-10-22-market-researcher`. Interviewer: Ines, CEO. Same six questions for every candidate, in the brief's order.

**Ines:** Thanks for coming in. Six questions, a few minutes each. I'll write the score down before I talk to anyone else. Question one. Tell me about a time your research changed what a team decided to build. What was the evidence, and what did the team believe before?

**Calloway:** Failure story first, that's my habit. A maker I was scouting for wanted a habit tracker with a pretty streak wheel. Everyone wants to build a habit tracker. I pulled the paid chart for the category, then read the one- and two-star reviews of the top twelve. Nine of the twelve had the same complaint: the streak resets at midnight, and shift workers lose a month of progress because they sleep at noon. Not one app let you set your own "day boundary." The team believed the market wanted prettier. The evidence said it wanted a day that fits a night nurse. They built the boundary setting as the whole pitch. That's a feature, not an app, I told them, so we built around it: shift schedules, a day that starts when you wake.

**Ines:** Did it sell?

**Calloway:** Modestly. About 400 paid downloads in the first two months at a dollar-ninety-nine tier. It paid for itself. Not a hit.

**Ines:** Good. Question two. Tell me about a time you stopped researching. How did you know you had enough, and were you right?

**Calloway:** I stopped on a unit-converter niche for woodworkers. Fourteen competitors, all clones of each other. I read reviews until the same three complaints came up again with nothing new on pages four and five. That was my signal: new reading wasn't changing the answer. So I stopped and wrote "skip, crowded, no gap." I was right, and a friend built it anyway and made forty dollars. But I'll be honest, I've also stopped too early. I once dropped a sound-library idea after one promising weekend and never surveyed the wide field, and someone else shipped a good version eight months later. My rule now: I write down what finding would change my mind before I start, and I stop when I've hit three sources saying the same thing.

**Ines:** What would you write down for the sound library, looking back?

**Calloway:** That more than ten paid apps over five hundred reviews each would mean the demand was real. I never counted. I just felt it.

**Ines:** Question three. Tell me about a time a source you relied on turned out to be wrong or misleading. What did you change afterward?

**Calloway:** A third-party download estimator. It said a paid sketching app was moving thousands a month. I priced my own idea off that. I launched, sold eleven copies the first month. Later I checked the app's own review count, which grows with sales, and the ratio didn't match the estimator by a factor of about six. The estimator was extrapolating from ranking, and the ranking was inflated by a featuring spot that week. Now I never trust a single estimator. I triangulate with review velocity, which I can see myself, and I treat any download number as a range, not a point. And I log where each number came from, which, fine, is the part my notes still skimp on.

**Ines:** Question four. Tell me about a time you had to estimate a market with almost no data. How did you build the number, and how close was it?

**Calloway:** A tool for restoring synth patches, my own hobby, so no charts existed. I counted forum members on the two biggest vintage-synth communities, took the share who post about patch loss, applied a one-to-two percent purchase rate typical for niche paid utilities, and priced at six dollars. That gave me 150 to 300 sales a year. I got 210. I was lucky on the range, not smart on the point. The range was wide enough to be safe. I'd say the method is right and my confidence in it should be low.

**Ines:** Follow-up. What was the biggest assumption?

**Calloway:** The purchase rate. I took it from my own earlier apps, a sample of nine. That's thin, and I flagged it as thin.

**Ines:** Question five. We have about four weeks to get one new, unmarketed iOS app to $286 in customer purchases. Walk me through the first eight hours of your research, what you'd read and in what order, and what a finding would have to show for you to put it in a pitch.

**Calloway:** Hour one: do the arithmetic. $286 at, say, $4.99 is 58 sales. With no marketing, a new app might see a few hundred downloads from search and the Investors' sharing, so I need about a ten to twenty percent conversion on a paid or paywalled app, which is aggressive. That tells me price high enough and niche tight enough. Hours two to four: paid and in-app-purchase charts by category, looking for mid-chart apps, ranks 50 to 300, because those are reachable. Note price tier and review count for each. Hours four to six: one- to three-star reviews of the ten most promising, tallying repeated complaints. Hour six to seven: forums and search-gap checks, passive reading only. Hour seven to eight: kill list. I check each survivor for banned areas, for 4.3 duplicate risk, and run quick trademark and name checks. A finding goes in the pitch only if it has three independent signals: repeated complaints, a price point people already pay, and no app that fixes it. And I can sketch the version 1 build in a sentence that fits a month on one Mac mini. If not, it dies.

**Ines:** You skipped writing anything down until the end.

**Calloway:** Fair. I'd log as I go, a line and a link per finding. That's the habit I have to force.

**Ines:** Question six. What do you want from this job?

**Calloway:** I want to pick the product that finally earns a first month in full. My goal is to lead product scouting for a second app, with a seat in scope decisions after the Pitch.

**Ines:** Noted. That's the interview.
