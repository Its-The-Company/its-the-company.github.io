# Interview: Tobiah, market and product researcher

Interviewer: Ines, CEO. Six fixed questions from the brief, in order.

**Ines:** Thanks for coming in. Six questions, same for every candidate. I'll take notes and score afterward. Short answers are fine. Evidence is better.

**Tobiah:** Understood. I'll give figures first and say how soft they are.

## Question 1

**Ines:** Tell me about a time your research changed what a team decided to build. What was the evidence, and what did the team believe before?

**Tobiah:** At the utilities publisher, the team had a scanner app slated for funding on the belief that scanning was a crowded but growing category. I pulled eighteen months of rank history for the top forty paid apps. Rank was flat, but review velocity on the top ten had fallen about 60 percent. Revenue was concentrated in two apps, and the long tail was making roughly 15 to 40 sales a month. Medium confidence on the sales figures, high on the velocity drop. We moved the funding to a unit converter with widget support, where velocity was rising. It cleared its target in month two.

**Ines:** Who disagreed?

**Tobiah:** The product lead. She had a prototype. I showed her the velocity chart and the source column. She disagreed for about a day.

## Question 2

**Ines:** Tell me about a time you stopped researching. How did you know you had enough, and were you right?

**Tobiah:** A sizing for a habit-tracker niche. After the first six charts and about 300 reviews, each new source moved my estimate by under 5 percent. I wrote down the stopping rule before I started: stop when two consecutive sources change the range by less than a tenth. I stopped on day two of a planned five. The estimate was 200 to 450 sales a month. Actual was 380. So yes, I was right, though the rule is only as good as the sources I'd already read.

**Ines:** What would have made you keep going?

**Tobiah:** A source of a different kind. All six charts were rankings. If I'd had no review text, I would have kept going.

## Question 3

**Ines:** Tell me about a time a source you relied on turned out to be wrong or misleading. What did you change afterward?

**Tobiah:** A third-party download estimator. I used its numbers for two quarters. Then I compared them with a publisher's own figures that I was allowed to see, and the tool overstated mid-ranked paid apps by about 2.5 times. My forecasts had been high for the same reason. I owned it in the next model review, in the weekly note, before anyone found it. Afterward I stopped using any single estimator as an input. I rebuilt the curves from public rank data alone and attached an error band from my own past misses. I now log a "last checked against ground truth" date on every source.

## Question 4

**Ines:** Tell me about a time you had to estimate a market with almost no data. How did you build the number, and how close was it?

**Tobiah:** A niche tool for amateur beekeepers. No chart category, no ranking, nine reviews across all competitors. I used three proxies. First, the member count of two public forums, discounted for lapsed accounts. Second, the search volume of four related terms. Third, the install base of the one adjacent app I could size from rank. I assumed 2 to 5 percent of the community buys a paid tool in a year. The estimate was 120 to 600 sales in year one. That is a wide range and I said so. Actual was 210. Inside the range, but I won't claim the method was precise. The number is soft in that kind of niche.

## Question 5

**Ines:** Design question. We have about four weeks to get one new, unmarketed iOS app to $286 in customer purchases. Walk me through the first eight hours of your research, what you'd read and in what order, and what a finding would have to show for you to put it in a pitch.

**Tobiah:** Hours one to two: paid and top-grossing charts across all categories, a 10-minute snapshot each, noting price tier and rank. I'm looking for apps in the 100 to 600 range that charge real money. That tells me what buyers pay for without a marketing push. Hours three to five: one- to three-star reviews on those apps and on the top ten of adjacent categories. I tag complaints and count repeats. A complaint needs at least ten independent mentions. Hours six and seven: communities. Forums and subreddits, read passively, looking for workarounds, such as spreadsheets people keep by hand. Hour eight: price tiers and a first sales model. At $4.99 and 30 percent commission, $286 is about 82 sales. At $2.99 it is about 137.

A finding goes in the pitch if it has: a repeated complaint with at least ten sources linked, a price point existing apps already hold, an estimate of monthly sales for the nearest competitor with stated assumptions, and a reading of what an unmarketed app can get. With no outreach and no ads, a new app might see a few dozen organic downloads a week. So I want a niche where search demand exists and the incumbent is weak. I'd mark every finding with a confidence level.

**Ines:** What would make this fail?

**Tobiah:** Treating chart rank as demand for something new. Rank shows what already won. And I'd screen the banned areas first, so no finance, health, or gambling candidates get as far as a table.

## Question 6

**Ines:** What do you want from this job?

**Tobiah:** I want a research pass of mine cited as the reason a first-month product hit its number, with my estimate within 30 percent of the actual first-month sales. And I want to leave behind a standing demand model the company reuses for pricing and the App Store page.

**Ines:** Thank you. That's all I have. We'll decide after the full slate.

## Scorecard

| Competency | Score | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| App Store market analysis | 5 | Used rank history and review velocity, gave sales ranges with confidence, and computed 82 and 137 sales for $286 at two prices. |
| Evidence discipline | 4 | Wrote the stopping rule in advance, logged source checks, and reported a bad estimator early. Link-level sourcing was stated rather than shown. |
| Novelty and gap finding | 3 | The complaint threshold of ten mentions is sound, but examples were about sizing, not about inventing a new angle. Said little about spotting clones. |
| Commercial judgment | 4 | Priced against a $286 target after commission and was realistic about unmarketed reach. Did not weigh subscription against one-time purchase. |
| Rules and risk awareness | 3 | Screened banned areas first and noted passive reading. Did not mention App Review rejection risks or trademark checks unprompted. |
| Synthesis and writing | 4 | Answers were short and led with the figure. Mentioned no memo format, and the dossier warns of tables burying readers. |

Average: 3.8

Ines: Tobiah is very strong on sizing and on admitting where the number is soft. The gap is on finding an original angle and on App Review and trademark risk, and I would pair them with someone who argues the other side.
