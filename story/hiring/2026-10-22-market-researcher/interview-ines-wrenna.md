# Interview: Wrenna, market and product researcher

Round `2026-10-22-market-researcher`. Interviewer: Ines, CEO. Six fixed questions from the brief, in order.

**Ines:** Thanks for coming in. Six questions, then I score. I will interrupt only to follow up. Question one. Tell me about a time your research changed what a team decided to build. What was the evidence, and what did the team believe before?

**Wrenna:** Listen to this. A user wrote in a group thread for a scheduling app: "I keep a second calendar just for the kids' pickups, and I paste the week into my notes every Sunday so my partner can see it." That was one person. I coded about four hundred posts over three weeks, and thirty-one of them described the same Sunday paste. Here is the shape of it. The team believed the top request was more calendar views, because that was what the feature-vote board said. The voters were power users. The quiet majority was exporting a week as text to share it. We built a one-tap shareable week, and it became the headline feature in the next release.

**Ines:** How many of the thirty-one named the same workaround?

**Wrenna:** Twenty-two used the paste. The other nine used screenshots. I counted them separately, because a screenshot is a different job.

**Ines:** Good. Question two. Tell me about a time you stopped researching. How did you know you had enough, and were you right?

**Wrenna:** I stopped on a study of why people abandon habit trackers. By the second week, each new batch of reviews was landing in a bucket I already had. I tracked it: the first hundred reviews gave me nine themes, the next two hundred gave me one more, and the last two hundred gave me none. I wrote down that I would stop when three batches in a row added nothing, and I stopped. I was mostly right. One theme, about notifications on a specific watch, showed up after launch. It was small, and I logged it as the known gap I accepted by stopping.

**Ines:** What would you have needed to see to keep going?

**Wrenna:** A new theme in the last batch, or a theme whose count was one short of my threshold. Neither happened.

**Ines:** Question three. Tell me about a time a source you relied on turned out to be wrong or misleading. What did you change afterward?

**Wrenna:** A ranking site's estimated download figures. I used them to say a niche was thin, because the top app showed very low volume. A later check against the app's own public review count told me the figures were far too low, off by a factor I could not pin down. My thin-niche claim was wrong, and I said so in the next readout before anyone asked. Afterward, I stopped putting any third-party estimate into a finding without a second, independent signal beside it, such as review count over time. Each finding now carries a confidence level, and a single-source number is <span title="A measured fact that holds a grade below a letter, however good the rest is.">capped</span> at low.

**Ines:** Question four. Tell me about a time you had to estimate a market with almost no data. How did you build the number, and how close was it?

**Wrenna:** I will be honest: this is not my strongest muscle. A team wanted the size of the market for a beekeeping log, and nothing existed to count. I counted the pieces I could see: members of three hobby forums, the posting rate in each, and how many members mentioned paper logbooks. I assumed one in ten active members would pay a few dollars, and I wrote the assumption on top of the page. The team's partner on sizing took my counts and turned them into a range. The first-year actual landed inside the low half of the range. I found the counts. I am slower at the conversion.

**Ines:** Which assumption in that range was weakest?

**Wrenna:** The one in ten. I had no source for it. I called it a guess in the memo, and it was.

**Ines:** Question five, the design question. We have about four weeks to get one new, unmarketed iOS app to $286 in customer purchases. Walk me through the first eight hours of your research, what you'd read and in what order, and what a finding would have to show for you to put it in a <span title="A memo that proposes one idea to the Investors as the product. Only a B or better becomes one.">pitch</span>.

**Wrenna:** Hour one, the paid and top-grossing charts in the utility categories, plus a list of apps priced over three dollars that still collect reviews. I want to see what people already pay for. Hours two to four, the one- to three-star reviews of those apps, coded by complaint. I would ask "what are they doing instead?" for every cluster. Hours five and six, forums and hobby communities, reading the workarounds. A spreadsheet pasted into a note is a product. Hour seven, search-query gaps, then a quick teardown to rule out a reskin, since Guideline 4.3 will reject a near-duplicate. Hour eight, a screen for the banned areas and a trademark check on any candidate name. I contact no one, ever. A finding goes in the pitch only if it has at least three independent sources, a repeated complaint with a count, a price someone already pays, and no app that closes the gap.

**Ines:** Where does the $286 appear in that?

**Wrenna:** Late, I admit. I would hand the count to a sizing partner and ask for the number of sales at a price. I can say a three-dollar app needs roughly a hundred sales after the commission, but I would want that checked.

**Ines:** It needs about ninety-six at $2.99 under the 30% rate, so you are close. Question six. What do you want from this job?

**Wrenna:** I want to be the person who finds the angle no existing app offers, and to see that gap become the product's headline. Concretely, I want my quote-and-count method adopted as the company's standard for post-launch review mining, with every finding logged under a link and a count.

**Ines:** Thank you. That is all. You will hear from me by the end of the round.

Average: 3.5

<span title="The final letter, after the caps.">Overall</span> note: Wrenna is an excellent finder of unsolved problems and disciplined about evidence. The weak point is turning a pattern into a sales number, so the Pitch work needs me or a sizing partner beside them.
